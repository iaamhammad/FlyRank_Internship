Can Search Position Patterns Predict Which Pages Need Content Refreshes?

Hammad Muhammad · FlyRank ML Internship · Lane 2 — Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring · September 2026
GitHub repository · Data: FlyRank Internship Warehouse

Abstract

Content editors at SEO agencies routinely face a triage problem: with thousands of pages across dozens of client accounts, which ones most urgently need a refresh, expansion, or protection review? This study asks whether observable search-position and engagement signals—available at the time of decision—can predict within-month impression decline with enough precision to meaningfully triage a content queue. Using 32,596 anonymized page-level records from the FlyRank internship warehouse (March 2026), I compare a transparent rule baseline (visible × slipped × depth, precision@50 = 0.36) against a depth-3 decision tree trained on six leakage-free features, evaluated on a client-grouped holdout of six unseen clients. The decision tree achieves a precision@50 of 0.48 on held-out clients (vs. 0.36 for the rule and 0.34 base rate), with a repeated 5-seed mean of 0.304—a real but modest and variable signal. These results address a concrete FlyRank content problem: giving editorial teams a principled queue so they spend sprint hours on the pages most likely to benefit from a refresh—not on pages already holding position—with position depth confirmed as the strongest available triage signal.

1. Introduction

An SEO agency managing 30 client accounts can have over 30,000 indexed pages in its portfolio at any moment. A single account can carry 7,000 pages. No editorial team can review them all in a sprint cycle. The real question is not which pages are declining—that's answerable from analytics—but which ones should someone look at first, given limited time this week?

This is a triage and ranking problem, not a prediction problem. The output is a ranked queue with reason codes, designed to focus human editorial judgment. A wrong recommendation has a real cost in both directions: a false positive wastes writer hours on a page that was already fine; a false negative lets a genuinely declining page lose traffic quietly until the next audit cycle catches it.

Prior to this work, the standard approach was either gut feel, recency (oldest publish date first), or a hand-coded rule based on position and volume thresholds. The reference pipeline in this repo (scripts/01–05) establishes that a trained model can outperform a hand-written rule on a random holdout. The question I pursue here is whether that advantage holds when using a client-grouped holdout—the deployment-realistic scenario where the model scores pages for clients it has never seen in training.

This is FlyRank's content refresh triage problem in its practical form: an SEO platform managing hundreds of client websites needs a repeatable, auditable method to surface the highest-priority pages for editorial action each sprint—pages where a timely refresh or expansion can recover lost search impressions before traffic erosion compounds. My findings show that a shallow, interpretable decision tree trained on position and engagement signals can outperform expert-designed rules on new clients, while making the reasons for every recommendation transparent to editors.

2. Data

Source

All data comes from the FlyRank internship warehouse (hf://datasets/FlyRank/internship-warehouse), a research dataset derived from real production search analytics. I used two tables:

  • fact_content_daily_performance/month=2026-03/*.parquet — daily GSC and GA4 metrics per page per day, March 2026.
  • dim_content.parquet — page-level metadata including content creation and last-update dates.

The warehouse covers data described as equivalent to roughly 79 million row-level observations across its full history. I work with one month's page-level aggregate (March 2026), yielding 32,596 rows after filters.

Population and filters

Snapshot date: 2026-03-31. I aggregate all daily rows in March 2026 to one page-level record per (client_hash_id, content_hash_id). I require ga4_data_available IS TRUE AND gsc_data_available IS TRUE to ensure both data sources are present. Pages with fewer than 100 total GSC impressions in March are excluded (too little signal to reliably define a decline within a single month). Pages with a missing avg_position are dropped (171 rows, <1%).

What was excluded and why

  • trend_direction and trend_pct — these columns define the label; using them as features would be direct leakage.
  • Raw page URLs and client domain names — the dataset uses anonymized hash IDs throughout. No identifying information appears anywhere in this analysis.
  • April 2026 data — used only in the validation audit to test temporal generalization. Not part of the training or test split.
  • content_updated_date as a raw date field — forward-dated update timestamps can introduce temporal leakage. Clipped to GREATEST(days_since_last_update, 0) and verified in w06.
Public-safety confirmation: no client names, domain URLs, raw search queries, or personally identifiable information appear anywhere in this paper or its associated notebooks.

3. Methodology

Label definition

is_declining_label = 1 if a page's total GSC impressions in the second half of March 2026 (days 16–31) are strictly less than in the first half (days 1–15). This is a pragmatic within-month proxy for "needs attention," not a confirmed long-run trend.

Feature contract (6 leakage-free features)

FeatureDescription
avg_position_marAverage Google Search Console position across March.
log_gsc_impressions_marlog(1 + impressions) — search demand volume.
engagement_rate_marGA4 engaged sessions ÷ total sessions × 100.
has_engagementBinary flag: whether GA4 tracked any sessions.
content_age_daysDays elapsed since initial page publication.
days_since_last_updateDays since last content modification, clamped ≥ 0.

Rule baseline

The reference baseline flags a page if it has ≥ 500 impressions (meaningful demand), average position ≥ 10 (fallen off page one or deep), and recorded engagement. Reason code: position_risk_with_demand. This mirrors real-world agency triage rules and sets a fair comparison point.

Validation design

I used a client-grouped holdout split (80% train / 20% test: 24 training clients, 6 held-out test clients; 4,495 test rows). The grouping is critical: in production, the model is deployed on new clients. A standard random row split leaks client-level baseline distributions into training, which inflated Random Forest AUC from 0.50 to 0.74 in experiments—an artifact, not generalization. I ran the same split over 5 random seeds to check stability.

Models evaluated

Three candidates: Logistic Regression (L2), Decision Tree (max_depth=3, class_weight='balanced', min_samples_leaf=50), and Random Forest (100 estimators). The decision tree was selected as the keeper because its decision rules are fully inspectable by agency stakeholders and it outperformed alternatives on precision@K on unseen clients.

Key finding: position is the signal; volume is only a gate

Signal audit (w04) measured decline rate by position bucket and impression tier independently. Position depth shows a monotone relationship: pages at positions 1–3 decline at 13.8%; at positions 21–50, the rate rises to 42.9%. Volume is nearly flat across tiers (25–28% decline rate), confirming that impressions function as a demand gate but carry no independent decline signal beyond that threshold.

One negative result worth noting: days_since_last_update showed median = 0 in March 2026 data due to a bulk CMS touch across multiple clients, providing near-zero discriminative power in this specific window. The feature stays in the contract because it's theoretically important and expected to activate in older data.

5. Limitations & Honest Framing

Every result here comes with conditions. I'll state them plainly:

  • Within-month label proxy. is_declining_label captures whether impressions fell from the first half to the second half of a single month. It is not a confirmed long-run trend. A page with a within-month dip may recover the following week.
  • Single month of data. Training and evaluation both use March 2026 only. The validation audit (w06) tested the model on April 2026 data and found performance degrades—particularly with the time-aware split (tree p@50 drops from 0.48 to 0.34).
  • High variance from small client count. 30 clients, with 6 held out, means split composition has an outsized effect on results. The 5-seed mean p@50 of 0.304 carries a range of 0.10–0.46—a 4× spread.
  • No causal design. I observe an association between search position and within-month impression change. No A/B refresh experiment was run. This tool supports triage decisions; it does not predict the outcome of a content refresh.
  • No generalisation claim beyond this portfolio. The dataset covers one anonymized portfolio for one month. Results may not transfer to different industries, page types, languages, or client sizes.
  • The model does not predict Google's algorithm. It predicts patterns in this dataset that correlate with within-month impression change. It makes no claim about ranking logic.
All findings in this paper use directional, decision-support language: "observed," "measured," "associated with," "suggests." Causal language has been avoided throughout.

6. What to Do With This

The action playbook maps page archetypes to editorial actions, in priority order. A content strategist can pull the top-N rows from the ranked queue each sprint cycle and schedule work by archetype.

  1. Review high-demand deep pages immediately. Pages with ≥ 3,000 impressions and avg. position ≥ 10—3,245 pages with a 48.9% decline rate, all rule-flagged. Action: refresh_review with intent audit + SERP check. A human must verify query intent is still being served.
  2. Work the low-demand deep queue in batches of 25–50. 10,787 pages at position ≥ 10 with < 3,000 impressions; 34.8% decline rate. Confirm the target query still has live search demand before investing refresh effort.
  3. Cap reviews at the top 25–50 rows per sprint. Precision drops from 0.48 at the top 50 to 0.41 at the top 100. Re-score the queue each cycle rather than working through a stale ranked list.
  4. Include model-only flagged pages as secondary candidates. 9,329 pages score ≥ 0.5 on the decision tree but fall below the rule threshold. Filter to the aging_page_one archetype (position < 10, age > 180 days, low engagement)—22.4% decline rate.
  5. Retrain when conditions shift. Trigger retrain if: (a) base rate shifts > 5pp from 26.8%; (b) model age exceeds 2 months; or (c) p@50 on a recent validation slice drops below 0.30.

What should not be automated

The ranked queue is a decision-support tool, not an automation trigger. An editor must: confirm query intent is still live; check whether a position change reflects a SERP feature displacement vs. a genuine ranking drop; and make the final call on whether to refresh, expand, protect, prune, or monitor a given page.
